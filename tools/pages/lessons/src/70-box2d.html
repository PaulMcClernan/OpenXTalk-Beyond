A world that moves by itself

Box2D is the physics engine inside many of the best-known 2D games: it moves things the way real things move, falling, bouncing, stacking, swinging and knocking each other over. The xTalk Suite's Box2Dxt brings it to xTalk, with handlers that begin with b2. This lesson uses Box2D's own handlers, which work in metres and radians; the next, Games with the Box2Dxt Kit, uses the Kit, which works in pixels and moves the controls of a card for you.

Three ideas carry it all. A world holds everything, with one gravity. Bodies are the things in it, and shapes give them their form and what they are made of. Then you step the world, and each step moves time on a little.

A world and a falling ball

b2NewWorld makes a world, given its gravity across and up: Box2D's y goes up, so -10 is ten metres a second, every second, down. (The two trues let bodies at rest fall asleep, which costs nothing, and keep fast bodies from passing through walls.) b2NewBody makes a body: its world, its type (2 is dynamic: gravity moves it), where it is in metres, and its angle. b2AddCircle gives it a round shape, half a metre in radius. Each b2Step moves time on by the seconds you give, 1/60 for a game running at 60 frames a second:

Every second the ball falls 10 metres a second faster, as a dropped stone does. b2DestroyWorld frees the world and everything in it when you are done.

Ground, and three kinds of body

A body's type says how it moves. A static body (0) never moves: floors, walls, shelves. A kinematic body (1) moves at the speed you give it with b2SetVelocity, and nothing pushes it: lifts and moving platforms. A dynamic body (2) is moved by gravity and by whatever it hits. What things are made of

The last three numbers of a shape are its density (how heavy it is for its size), its friction (how it grips) and its restitution, its bounce: 0 is a lump of clay, 1 a perfect rubber ball. Pushes and kicks

To make a body move, give it a speed with b2SetVelocity, push it over time with b2ApplyForce, or kick it once with b2ApplyImpulse, which changes its speed at once by the impulse divided by its mass. Joints

A joint ties two bodies together. A revolute joint is a hinge: the bodies may turn about one point, as a door on its hinge or a pendulum on its pin. b2RevoluteJoint takes the world, the two bodies, the hinge's place on each (in each body's own metres) and whether they may collide. A hinge can have limits and a motor, and Box2Dxt has the other joints too: distance joints (a rod, or a spring), sliders, wheels with suspension, welds and ropes. The Dictionary lists them all.

Contacts and rays

A game needs to know when things touch: a ball landing, a player reaching a coin. After each step, b2ContactsUpdate takes a snapshot of what began to touch, ended and hit hard in that step, and b2ContactBeginBodyA and b2ContactBeginBodyB say which two bodies began. A ray asks what lies along a line, as a laser or a line of sight would: b2CastRayClosest says whether it hit, and b2RayBody and b2RayY say what and where:

Drawing it on a card

Box2D only works out where things are; drawing them is up to you. On a card, a graphic for each body, moved to its body's place after each step, is enough. A metre has to become some pixels, and Box2D's y, which goes up, has to become a card's, which goes down: here a metre is 40 pixels, and the point 0,0 of the world is at the middle of the card's bottom. This builds a little tower and a ball beside it, lets them settle for two seconds, and draws them where they came to rest:

A game does the same 60 times a second, from a timer (Animation and games), and sets each graphic's points from its body's angle too, so that it turns. The Kit does all of this for you, which is why most games use it; Box2D's own handlers are there when you want full control. Try it yourself

A ball rolls off a table 1 metre high at 3 metres a second. Where does it land? Make the table a static box whose top is at height 1, start the ball on it moving at 3 across, and step until the ball's bottom touches the floor; put how far it landed from the table's edge.

Show a solution

A body falls 1 metre in about 0.45 seconds whatever its speed across, so at 3 metres a second it lands a little over 1.3 metres out. The table has no friction, so the ball slides rather than rolls.
